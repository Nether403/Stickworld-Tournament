# Stickworld Tournament: Recommended 10-Game Launch Roster

**Prepared by Manus AI**  
**Date:** 18 August 2026

## Executive recommendation

**Stickworld Tournament should launch as a portfolio of short, legible physics competitions rather than as ten unrelated minigames.** The recommended roster combines five immediately understandable live PvP events with three asynchronous skill disciplines and two team/objective games. Every title uses the same stickman silhouette language, competition account, cosmetic system, replay viewer, seasonal divisions, and tournament calendar; the competitive verbs nevertheless remain distinct: fight, aim, swing, traverse, shoot, score, shove, deliver, evade, and balance.

The closest market precedents validate the underlying ingredients without defining the product. *Stick Fight: The Game* demonstrates the appeal of short multiplayer physics combat, interactive levels, many weapons, and procedural animation. [1] *Ragdoll Archers* shows how readable projectile trajectories, ricochets, and reactive characters can make aiming expressive. [2] *Stickman Hook* centres its skill expression on grappling, release timing, momentum, bumpers, and obstacle avoidance. [3] *Vex* demonstrates tight stickman traversal through traps, checkpoints, and challenge variants. [4] *Rooftop Snipers* shows that a chaotic competitive duel can remain accessible with only jump and shoot controls. [5]

> **Creative boundary:** These are original concepts inspired by high-level design patterns only. Stickworld Tournament should not reproduce another game’s characters, artwork, names, maps, exact controls, sound, animations, level layouts, code, or progression economy.

## Concept cards

### 1. Crownfall Clash

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Crownfall Clash is the platform’s headline 2–4 player arena brawler. A contestant runs, jumps, grabs ledges, shoves, performs a charged strike, and uses temporary arena tools to score ring-outs. Damage is represented by an escalating instability meter: the fuller it is, the farther a hit launches the stickman. Arenas contain readable but consequential physics—crumbling bridge panels, tilting platforms, pendulums, water wheels, low-gravity vents, and moving cover. A 90-second round ends when one player reaches the ring-out target or when the score leader survives sudden death.

For **ranked solo competition**, use a four-person rating queue and a separate 1v1 duel ladder. Players advance through divisions using placement-weighted rating, with explicit penalties for intentional disconnects. For **tournaments**, run four-player qualifier lobbies, then seed the top two into a final; invite-only events can use double-elimination 1v1. The **leaderboard** should show rating, top-four rate, ring-outs per round, and a curated library of server-authorized highlight replays. A solo survival version uses the same arenas with escalating hazards, producing a daily leaderboard without polluting the PvP rating.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** The high-level reference is *Stick Fight: The Game*, which officially describes 2–4 player online/local physics combat, highly interactive levels, weapons, and procedural animations. [1] Adopt its readability principle—fast rounds where a changing arena matters—but replace its specific weapon ecosystem with a neutral **Arena Tool Draft**. At each round start, players vote between three temporary tool families, such as grapple glove, shock baton, or foam cannon, each constructed around knockback and counterplay rather than lethal damage.

### 2. Arrowline Arena

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Arrowline Arena is a 1v1 terrain-dueling game built around charged shots. Players select an angle and draw strength while observing wind ribbons, destructible shields, moving targets, and bounce surfaces. Both contestants commit within a short planning window; arrows then resolve at once, making prediction and baiting central. A direct head or torso hit scores high, limb hits cause brief aim sway, and environmental trick shots earn controlled bonus points. A match is first to five points or highest score after three minutes.

For **ranked solo competition**, the primary ladder is 1v1 seasonal rating, supplemented by a solo **Range Circuit** that scores a fixed sequence of targets on accuracy, speed, and trick-shot multiplier. **Tournament play** uses best-of-three maps with mirrored wind conditions and fixed loadouts; a Swiss qualifier feeds a single-elimination top eight. The **leaderboard** separates duel rating from weekly circuit score and preserves shot-path replays so top performances are inspectable.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** *Ragdoll Archers* is an appropriate mechanical reference because its official listing emphasizes accurate arrow trajectories, ricochets, dynamic environmental interaction, and reactive character motion. [2] Retain only the general lesson that ballistics must be readable. Arrowline should use a cleaner, tournament-first silhouette: no stat upgrades in ranked modes, no paid arrow advantage, fixed competitive bow archetypes, wind forecasts visible to both contestants, and a brief pre-shot aim line only in novice playlists.

### 3. Gripline Grand Prix

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Gripline Grand Prix is a 4–6 player aerial race through a vertical course. Each stickman can run, air-dash once, attach a grappling line to approved anchors, reel slightly, and release. The winning route is rarely a straight line: a player can take a safe chain of anchors, risk a long pendulum swing, use bounce pads, or slingshot from a moving crane. Contact causes modest disruption rather than full knockouts, so the game rewards route execution more than griefing.

For **ranked solo competition**, head-to-head heat placement awards rating and a separate **Time Attack** records fastest verified laps. **Tournament play** begins with six-player heats, advances the top three to semifinals, and ends in a four-player multi-lap final. The **leaderboard** features seasonal bests by track, a ghost overlay for the current leader, and daily track seeds with a limited number of ranked attempts. The single-player route is therefore meaningfully competitive, not merely practice against AI.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** The reference lesson from *Stickman Hook* is its rhythm of grappling, releasing, jumping, using bumpers, avoiding obstacles, and carrying momentum through a course. [3] Stickworld should not make a level-by-level clone. Instead, it should make anchors part of a fair race system: every ranked track has authored anchor geometry, normalised rope length, server-defined moving-platform timing, alternate risk routes, and collision rules that prevent one bump from invalidating a full run.

### 4. Trapwire Trials

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Trapwire Trials is a precision platforming discipline in which a stickman runs, vaults, wall-slides, ledge-grabs, rolls, and triggers limited-use recovery tools. Courses are compact obstacle puzzles built around saw lanes, collapsing slabs, timed shutters, pressure plates, water currents, moving lifts, and directional fans. Death sends the runner to a nearby checkpoint but adds a time penalty; a flawless run earns a clean-run bonus. The core skill is not memorisation alone: a runner must choose when to preserve momentum and when to reset safely.

For **ranked solo competition**, each day presents one fixed-seed featured course and each week a three-course **Triathlon**. The score blends elapsed time, deaths, collectable route flags, and clean-run bonus. **Tournament play** uses mirrored side-by-side courses for short duels, or a multi-round time-attack cup in which the slowest entrants are cut after each course. The **leaderboard** records best time, clean time, route medal count, game-build identifier, and verified replay, ensuring that a world record has auditable context.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** *Vex* is a useful reference for its emphasis on precise running, jumping, climbing, traps, checkpoints, and challenge variants. [4] The implementation translation is to keep all ranked course rules fixed for a season while rotating cosmetic themes. Avoid character power-ups; allow only clearly bounded recovery charges that are identical for all contestants. This preserves the one-more-run appeal while keeping the result attributable to execution.

### 5. Recoil Rooftops

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Recoil Rooftops is a 1v1 physics duel on small elevated maps. The entire competitive control set is intentionally compact: jump, aim, and charge/release a shot. Firing induces recoil; a missed heavy shot can throw the shooter backwards, while a well-timed jump-shot may clear an obstacle or alter the projectile arc. Each map cycles through two environmental states—such as gusty wind, moving antennae, loose signs, or collapsing parapets—so positioning matters as much as accuracy. The round winner is the first to force two ring-outs, or the player ahead when the overtime storm closes the safe space.

For **ranked solo competition**, players enter best-of-five rated sets. A **Rooftop Range** solo mode uses the same recoil model to score target sequences, enabling aim leaderboard play even when no opponent is online. **Tournament play** uses alternating map bans, mirrored spawns, and first-to-three sets; the top bracket is highly watchable because every decisive shot is visible and replayable. The **leaderboard** tracks duel rating, round conversion rate, and range-circuit best score rather than a purchase-driven power score.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** *Rooftop Snipers* demonstrates the accessibility of a chaotic local duel driven by jumping, holding a shot, and knocking an opponent off the map. [5] Keep the accessibility, but distinguish the game through aimable arcs, recoil self-risk, neutral wind, map-state rotation, and an explicit competitive ruleset. Its depth should arise from positional prediction and resource timing, not an imitation of the original game’s exact two-button feel.

### 6. Scrapball Showdown

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Scrapball Showdown is a fast 2v2 sport played in a compact, asymmetric salvage yard. Teams score by driving a heavy scrapball through the opponent’s magnetic gate. A player may carry the ball only briefly before it becomes unstable, encouraging passes, bounce shots, shoulder checks, and banked throws. Sticky walls, conveyor belts, spring ramps, and magnetic zones create understandable set plays. A round lasts three minutes; mercy rule and overtime prevent stalemates.

For **ranked solo competition**, every player queues individually but is matched into balanced two-person teams; rating changes reflect opponent strength, contribution-neutral win/loss, and anti-boosting safeguards. A solo **Shot Lab** scores a fixed pattern of bank shots, carry chains, and target gates. **Tournament play** is best as two-player squads with group play followed by a double-elimination bracket. Its **leaderboards** should recognize individual seasonal rating, squad rating, win streak, and a separate skill-lab score, rather than rewarding raw hours played.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** The design borrows the broad physics-comedy insight shared by *Stick Fight* and ragdoll stickman games: collisions, unstable bodies, and dynamic terrain can make a simple objective spectacular. [1] [2] The implementation should prioritise sport clarity over pure chaos. The ball must have a highly visible possession halo, collision materials must be consistent, and knockdowns need short predictable recovery windows so coordinated skill reliably beats random bumping.

### 7. Crate King

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Crate King is a pushout-and-construction contest. In a circular arena, players drag, shove, stack, and kick lightweight cargo crates to create ramps, cover, and elevated scoring platforms. Holding the central beacon earns points, but the beacon rises every 30 seconds, forcing contestants to build upward and destabilising old structures. A grab meter limits infinite defensive holds; a timed stomp can topple a stack but leaves the attacker exposed. The result is a legible contest for high ground rather than a generic fistfight.

For **ranked solo competition**, users may play 1v1 duels or four-player free-for-all, each with a separately calibrated rating. A single-player **Tower Ascent** challenge rewards the highest stable ascent within a fixed time and uses a daily crate sequence. **Tournament play** uses 1v1 best-of-three on symmetrical maps and four-player finals for showcase events. The **leaderboard** displays rating, beacon-control seconds, stack height, and verified tower-ascent score.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** This concept adapts the interactive-level lesson of *Stick Fight*—where the environment is part of the contest—rather than its combat specifics. [1] Originality comes from construction as the central verb. Use a constrained crate set with mass classes, visible stability outlines during practice, and a ranked rule that disables arbitrary community maps until they pass a physics and fairness certification.

### 8. Payload Panic

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Payload Panic is a 2v2 cargo-delivery sport. Neutral energy cells spawn at the centre and must be carried or thrown into a team’s delivery reactor. Cells have different weights and properties: light cells fly farther, heavy cells require two hands and slow a runner, volatile cells score extra but explode if held too long. Teammates can launch each other from spring plates, intercept passes, use temporary barricades, or steal a loose cell. The first team to a target score wins.

For **ranked solo competition**, individual players use a solo queue with role-neutral rating; private squads also receive a team rating. A **Relay Route** single-player challenge assesses pickup, throw, and movement efficiency through a fixed set of delivery gates. **Tournament play** supports two-player teams, round-robin pools, and a best-of-five grand final with map rotation. The **leaderboard** should distinguish individual and squad ranks, plus relay best times, so solo specialists and fixed teams both have a reason to compete.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** It uses the same general foundation established by physics-based stickman combat—expressive movement, collision, and interactive spaces—but moves the win condition to co-ordination and delivery. [1] Build the concept around information clarity: cells need colour-blind-safe icons, a visible fuse or weight indicator, server-confirmed pick-up ownership, and a short no-steal grace period after spawn to reduce opening randomness.

### 9. Fuse Frenzy

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** Fuse Frenzy is an elimination game in which a volatile core spawns with a clearly visible fuse. The holder can throw, hand off at close range, bat the core with arena tools, or launch it through pinball-like bumpers. When the fuse expires, the blast knocks out nearby contestants and damages the arena; the last stickman standing takes the round. Later rounds add falling platforms and shorter fuses, producing a clean escalation curve. The game is less about aim accuracy than about reading opponent routes, timing a throw, and maintaining an escape plan.

For **ranked solo competition**, players join four-person rated matches; placement across a series of rounds determines rating movement. A **Survival Gauntlet** provides the solo, leaderboard-driven version: increasingly difficult bot patterns and arena hazards measure time survived and cores defused. **Tournament play** runs four-player groups with points for each round placement, followed by a six-round final. The **leaderboard** shows rating, survival streak, average placement, and gauntlet score.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** The high-level influence is the fast, environmental, physics-first elimination loop seen in *Stick Fight*. [1] The original distinction is the shared, visible timer as the main strategic object. Competitive integrity depends on an exact server clock, uniform throw power limits, high-contrast fuse feedback, and a one-tick grace period after a handoff so network jitter cannot decide an elimination.

### 10. StickShift Trials

**Core gameplay and competitive structure.** StickShift Trials is a side-view stickman bike discipline. The player controls throttle, brake, lean, and a limited mid-air body shift. Courses reward clean landings, controlled wheelies, flips, and route choices across ramps, loose planks, seesaws, mud, ice, and breakable barriers. A crash does not necessarily end the run: the rider can recover at a penalty cost, which produces the satisfying risk decision between a safe route and a high-speed stunt line.

For **ranked solo competition**, this is the clearest asynchronous discipline: a daily authored route, weekly cup, personal best history, and ghost racing against nearby leaderboard entries. **Tournament play** consists of seeded heats in which athletes race asynchronously against the same server-timed course; the quickest verified times advance to knockout rounds. A live final can show all riders as simultaneous ghosts on a broadcast-friendly split view. The **leaderboard** tracks clean time, raw time, stunt score, route medal, physics version, and replay validation status.

**Precedent translated into an original game.** The reference is the broader stickman obstacle-racing lineage represented by *Vex*’s traversal-and-hazard formula and the Vex X3M bike series listed on the franchise site. [4] Rather than reproducing those layouts, StickShift should build a distinct torque-and-balance identity: the rider’s centre of mass, not merely vehicle speed, determines landing quality; every ranked track uses hand-authored terrain and a fixed simulation release.

## Why these ten work together

The portfolio avoids the two common launch failures of a minigame platform: putting every event behind synchronous matchmaking, and making every event a reskinned brawler. **Crownfall, Arrowline, Gripline, Trapwire, and Recoil Rooftops** establish the headline competitive identities. **Scrapball, Crate King, Payload Panic, and Fuse Frenzy** supply social and tournament variety. **StickShift Trials** provides an always-available high-skill progression surface when concurrency is low.

| Player need | Games serving it | Product implication |
|---|---|---|
| A match in under two minutes | Crownfall Clash, Recoil Rooftops, Fuse Frenzy | Prioritise instant queues and short rematches. |
| A pure individual skill test | Arrowline Range Circuit, Trapwire Trials, StickShift Trials | Offer fixed-seed daily events and verified replays. |
| Spectator-friendly tournament moments | Crownfall Clash, Arrowline Arena, Gripline Grand Prix, Fuse Frenzy | Build broadcast overlay, bracket, and replay tools early. |
| Co-operation without losing competition | Scrapball Showdown, Payload Panic | Support solo queue and registered squads separately. |
| Low-barrier, mobile-compatible controls | Recoil Rooftops, Arrowline Arena, Fuse Frenzy | Design keyboard, touch, and controller input parity from day one. |

## Competitive rules that apply to every title

A game should only enter ranked rotation after it has a fixed ruleset, versioned physics constants, symmetric or role-balanced spawns, deterministic event seed, server-signed result, and a replay payload sufficient to investigate a record. Cosmetic unlocks can celebrate status, but **ranked performance must never depend on purchased characters, permanent stat upgrades, or unannounced physics changes**. Seasonal maps and modes should be previewed in an unranked lab before promotion to the official circuit.

The result is a credible, varied opening roster: each game can stand alone, yet every new mode increases the value of a single Stickworld account, seasonal rating, cosmetic collection, friend group, and tournament audience.

## Web implementation blueprint

### Shared architecture

Build the ten games as a **TypeScript monorepo** with reusable competition services and narrowly scoped game modules. Matter.js is a credible common 2D simulation candidate because it is a web-oriented engine with rigid/compound bodies, collision handling, constraints, friction, gravity, and event hooks. [6] It should power physical objects, while the presentation layer uses a renderer such as Phaser 3 or PixiJS plus custom skeletal stickman drawing. A stickman should be represented visually by a small hierarchy of segments and joints, but its competitive collision hull should remain deliberately simple—typically capsule, circle, or box shapes—so the outcome is stable and easy to explain.

For synchronous ranked games, use Node.js game rooms with **server authority**. Colyseus is a suitable framework pattern because its documentation provides authoritative rooms, server-defined state synchronization, and matchmaking. [7] The server receives intents such as `move`, `jump`, `grab`, `aim`, `release`, and `useTool`; it validates each against stamina, cooldowns, position, and a fixed-timestep simulation before publishing the resulting state. The browser may predict its own movement and interpolate remote actors for responsiveness, but it must never decide an elimination, collision, score, finish time, or rating result.

| Layer | Recommended responsibility | Implementation decision |
|---|---|---|
| Browser shell | Account, lobby, tournament bracket, settings, UI, replay viewer | React + TypeScript with a responsive canvas host; keyboard, gamepad, and touch abstraction. |
| Game renderer | Camera, particles, stickman art, HUD, input sampling, prediction | Phaser 3 or PixiJS on Canvas/WebGL; procedural stick figure drawn from shared pose data. |
| Shared rules package | Maps, physics constants, input schema, collision categories, scoring formulas | Versioned TypeScript package; immutable release ID attached to every match and run. |
| Simulation room | Authoritative ticks, collision resolution, timers, bot fallback, result signing | Node.js TypeScript; Matter.js simulation for appropriate 2D modes; fixed timestep and explicit seed. |
| Competition API | Profiles, rating, events, brackets, results, cosmetics, moderation | REST or RPC service backed by PostgreSQL; transactional rating updates. |
| Fast-match services | Room discovery, presence, queue, transient snapshot fan-out, cache | Colyseus rooms with Redis presence / queue state and horizontal workers. |
| Durable storage | Matches, inputs, replays, leaderboard entries, audit history | PostgreSQL for relational records; object storage for compressed replays and thumbnails. |
| Operations | Metrics, abuse detection, build promotion, incident response | Structured event telemetry, error tracking, dashboards, feature flags, rollbackable physics versions. |

### Competitive integrity and leaderboard model

The platform should implement two authoritative paths rather than forcing all games through one network design. **Live PvP** uses server simulation at a fixed tick rate, client-side prediction only for local responsiveness, state snapshots for reconciliation, and a signed result at match end. **Asynchronous skill events** distribute a seeded course configuration, record a compact input stream, then re-simulate it on the server before the record is eligible for the leaderboard. Both paths require the same immutable identifiers: `game_id`, `ruleset_version`, `map_id`, `seed`, `client_build`, `input_hash`, `server_replay_hash`, and `event_window`.

> **Leaderboard acceptance rule:** A result is provisional until the server has either simulated the entire live match or replayed the submitted input stream against the exact event seed and ruleset. Only then may it affect rating, a bracket, or a public leaderboard.

| Safeguard | What it prevents | Product rule |
|---|---|---|
| Server authority | Forged scores, forged hit claims, altered timers | The client sends intent; only the simulation service produces results. |
| Fixed timestep and version pinning | Ambiguous world records after physics changes | Freeze tick rate, constants, map, and simulation release for each event window. |
| Seeded event configuration | Hand-edited daily courses and favourable random outcomes | Server issues signed seed and map variant; the run stores both. |
| Input-rate and state validation | Speed hacks, impossible movement, automation bursts | Reject actions beyond allowed cadence, cooldown, force, or position tolerance. |
| Replay verification | Unreviewable records and disputes | Persist compact input stream plus checkpoints; render server replay on demand. |
| Rating transactions | Duplicate wins, race-condition rank changes | Idempotent match-result key and a single atomic rating update. |
| Match protection | Disconnect abuse and collusion | Reconnect grace period, deterministic bot or forfeit policy, pair-frequency monitoring, and manual review queue. |

### Development sequence

A unified platform does not imply that all ten games must be developed independently. Build **three reusable primitives first**: a precision movement controller, a projectile/ballistics system, and a 2D object-constraint system. Crownfall, Recoil, Fuse Frenzy, Scrapball, Crate King, and Payload Panic then share much of the constraint/collision foundation. Arrowline shares the projectile stack; Gripline shares constrained movement; Trapwire and StickShift strengthen the verified asynchronous-run pipeline.

| Release wave | Games | Purpose | Exit criterion before ranked launch |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Foundation** | Trapwire Trials, Arrowline Arena, Recoil Rooftops | Establish input replay, time attack, ballistics, 1v1 rooms, and first seasonal ladder. | Server replay agrees with top-run outcome; input/control accessibility tests pass; ranked maps have symmetry review. |
| **Physics showcase** | Crownfall Clash, Gripline Grand Prix, Fuse Frenzy | Establish four-player rooms, constraints, spectator replays, and short-session retention. | Load tests meet target concurrency; disconnect policy and final-tick cases are covered by automated simulation tests. |
| **Social competition** | Scrapball Showdown, Crate King, Payload Panic | Add teams, parties, objectives, and deep interactive arenas. | 2v2 matchmaking maintains fair teams; item/stack edge cases are verified; observers see the same authoritative state. |
| **Always-on mastery** | StickShift Trials | Add vehicle physics and a headline daily record chase. | Server re-simulation validates ghost files at scale; track QA certifies each ranked route. |

### Scope discipline

The technically safest launch goal is a shared **2D side-view physics platform**, not ten engines. Do not introduce user-generated ranked maps, permanent gameplay stat upgrades, complicated character classes, cross-platform ranked parity promises, or large-scale battle royale modes in the first season. First prove that a player can load a game quickly, understand the objective in seconds, complete a credible ranked or solo-competitive attempt, watch a replay, and trust the result.
